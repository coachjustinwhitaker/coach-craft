Who Is in Front of Us

Before we talk about what to teach, we need to talk about who we are teaching. U11-U12 is one of the most complex developmental windows in a young person’s life. Understanding what is happening to your players off the pitch is just as important as understanding what you want them to do on it.

Social

These players are in the middle of one of the biggest transitions of their childhood, moving from the familiar structure of elementary school into the far less predictable world of middle school. They are beginning to assert their individuality and independence, but they are also intensely loyal to their peer group and deeply influenced by what those peers think of them. They are self-conscious about their abilities. They can be critical of adults, including you.

What this means for coaching

A player who feels judged in front of teammates will shut down. A player who feels safe will take risks. The training environment you create is not separate from their social experience. It is part of it.

Emotional

Joy, frustration, euphoria, and anger can cycle through a single training session at this age. These players are learning to handle strong emotions and they do not always manage it cleanly. Empathy is still developing. They are sensitive to the impressions of others and vulnerable to peer pressure. They do not accept authority blindly.

What this means for coaching

Emotional reactions on the field are not defiance or weakness. They are developmentally normal. Your steadiness in those moments teaches them something more valuable than any technical skill.

Cognitive

These players are eager to learn. Active listening is improving. They are beginning to think logically and can engage with abstract concepts like team shape, game state, and decision-making. They enjoy problem-solving. They like understanding why.

The challenge: self-regulation is still developing. They can act impulsively. They struggle to see all the options available to them, which directly affects decision-making under pressure. Complex multi-step instructions are hard to hold.

What this means for coaching

Short, clear questions beat long explanations. Constraint-based training beats lecturing. Let the game do the teaching whenever possible.

Physical

Growth rates are beginning to accelerate. Some players are entering puberty, others are not, and the physical differences between players on the same team can be significant. Players going through growth spurts can be more susceptible to overuse injuries. Physical qualities like speed and strength are improving, but unevenly across the group.

What this means for coaching

The biggest player is not always the most developed player. Do not confuse physical maturity with technical readiness. And watch training loads, especially for players going through growth spurts.

What US Soccer Asks of This Age Group

Foundation Phase III — U11-U12 Development Goals

The US Soccer Player Development Framework centers this age group on four goals: scanning and positioning, receiving under pressure, combination play, and finishing. These map directly to the B1-B5 blocks in this framework.

US Soccer coaching guidance for this phase is worth reading straight: be patient with players who are eager to learn. Find the appropriate level of challenge to stimulate without stifling. Help each player develop at their own rate without comparing them to others. Encourage multiple positions and roles throughout the season.

That is the standard. This site is built to help you meet it.

Source: US Soccer Player Development Framework, Foundation Phase III, U11-U12 Learning Plan (2023)
